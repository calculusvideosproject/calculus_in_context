    Section 6.4 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

    Subsection 6.4.1 Two Views of Power and Energy

    In section 1 we considered how much energy a town consumed over 24 hours when it was using \(p(t)\) megawatts of power at time \(t\text{.}\) Suppose \(E(T)\) megawatt-hours of energy were consumed during the first \(T\) hours.

    Aside
    Energy is the integral of power…
    🔗
    Then the integral, introduced in section 3, gave us the language to describe how \(E\) depends on \(p\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} E(T) = \int_0^T p(t) \, dt. \end{equation*}
    Because \(E\) is the integral of \(p\text{,}\) we can visualize \(E(T)\) as the area under the power graph \(y = p(t)\) as the time \(t\) sweeps from 0 hours to \(T\) hours:
    🔗
    As \(T\) increases, so does \(E(T)\text{.}\) The exact relation between \(E\) and \(T\) is shown in the graph below. The height of the graph of \(E\) at any point \(T\) is equal to the area under the graph of \(p\) from 0 out to the point \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The microscope window on the graph of \(E\) reminds us that the slope of the graph at any point \(T\) is

    Aside
    …and power is the derivative of energy
    🔗
    just \(p(T)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} p(T) = E'(T). \end{equation*}
    We discovered this fact in section 1, where we stated it in the following form: power is the rate at which energy is consumed.
    🔗
    Pause now to study the graphs of power and energy. You should convince yourself that the height of the \(E\) graph at any time equals the area under the \(p\) graph up to that time. For example, when \(T = 0\) no area has accumulated, so \(E(0) = 0\text{.}\) Furthermore, up to \(T = 6\) hours, power demand was almost constant at about 30 megawatts. Therefore, \(E(6)\) should be about
    \begin{equation*} 30 \ \mbox{megawatts} \times 6 \ \mbox{hours} = 180 \ \mbox{megawatt-hours}. \end{equation*}
    It is. You should also convince yourself that the slope of the \(E\) graph at any point equals the height of the \(p\) graph at that point. Thus, for example, the graph of \(E\) will be steepest where the graph of \(p\) is tallest.
    🔗
    Notice that when we write the energy accumulation function \(E(T)\) as an integral,
    \begin{equation*} E(T) = \int_0^T p(t) \, dt, \end{equation*}
    we have introduced a new ingredient.

    Aside
    A new ingredient: variable limits of integration
    🔗
    The time variable \(T\) appears as one of the “limits of integration.” By definition, the integral is a single number. However, that number depends on the interval of integration \([0, T]\text{.}\) As soon as we treat \(T\) as a variable, the integral itself becomes a variable, too. Here the value of the integral varies with \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We now have two ways of viewing the relation between power and energy. According to the first view, the energy accumulation function \(E\) is the integral of power demand:
    \begin{equation*} E(T) = \int_0^T p(t) \, dt. \end{equation*}
    According to the second view,

    Aside
    This integral is a solution to a differential equation
    🔗
    the energy accumulation function is the solution \(y = E(t)\) to an initial value problem defined by power demand:
    \begin{equation*} y' = p(t); \qquad y(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    If we take the first view, then we find \(E\) by refining Riemann sums—because that is the way to determine the value of an integral. If we take the second view, then we can find \(E\) by using any of the methods for solving initial value problems that we studied in chapter 4. Thus, the energy integral is a solution to a certain differential equation.
    🔗
    This is unexpected. Differential equations involve derivatives. At first glance, they have nothing to do with integrals. Nevertheless, the relation between power and energy shows us that there is a deep connection between derivatives and integrals.

    Aside
    The fundamental theorem of calculus
    🔗
    As we shall see, the connection holds for the integral of any function. The connection is so important—because it links together the two basic processes of calculus—that it has been called the fundamental theorem of calculus.
    🔗
    The fundamental theorem gives us a powerful new tool to calculate integrals. Our aim in this section is to see why the theorem is true, and to begin to explore its use as a tool. In Calculus II we will consider many specific integration techniques that are based on the fundamental theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.4.2 Integrals and Differential Equations

    We begin with a statement of the fundamental theorem for a typical function \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We have always been able to find the value of the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \end{equation*}
    by refining Riemann sums. The fundamental theorem gives us a new way. It says: First, find the solution \(y = A(x)\) to the initial value problem \(y' = f(x)\text{,}\) \(y(a) = 0\) using any suitable method for solving the differential equation. Then, once we have \(A(x)\text{,}\) we get the value of the integral by evaluating \(A\) at \(x = b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To see how all this works,

    Aside
    A test case
    🔗
    let’s find the value of the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^4 \cos(x^2) \, dx \end{equation*}
    two ways: by refining Riemann sums and by solving the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} y' = \cos(x^2); \quad y(0) = 0 \end{equation*}
    using Euler’s method.
    🔗
    To estimate the value of the integral, we use the program

    Aside
    RIEMANN versus…
    🔗
    RIEMANN from page . It produces the sequence of left endpoint sums shown in the table below on the left. Since the first three digits have stabilized, the value of the integral is \(.594\ldots\) . The integral was deliberately chosen to lead to the initial value problem we first considered on page . We solved that problem by Euler’s method, using the program

    Aside
    …TABLE
    🔗
    TABLE, and produced the table of estimates for the value of \(y(4)\) that appear in the table on the right. We see \(y(4) = .594\ldots\) .
    🔗

    Aside
    The two programs are the same
    🔗
    This speeds up the calculations but still gives us the final outcome.) Once you make necessary modifications (e.g., change x to t, a to tinitial, et cetera), you can see the two programs are the same.
    🔗
    The very fact that these two programs do the same thing gives us one proof of the fundamental theorem of calculus.
    🔗

    Graphing accumulation functions.

    Let’s take a closer look at the solution \(y = A(x)\) to the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} y' = \cos(x^2); \qquad y(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    We can write it as the accumulation function
    \begin{equation*} A(X) = \int_0^X \cos(x^2) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    By switching to the graphing version of TABLE (this is the program PLOT—see page ), we can graph \(A\text{.}\) Here is the result.
    🔗
    The relation between \(y = \cos(x^2)\) and \(y = A(x)\) is the same as the relation between power and energy.
    🔗
    • The height of the graph \(y = A(x)\) at any point \(x = X\) is equal to the signed area between the graph \(y = \cos(x^2)\) and the \(x\)-axis over the interval \(0 \leq x \leq X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • On the intervals where \(\cos(x^2)\) is positive, \(A(x)\) is increasing. On the intervals where \(\cos(x^2)\) is negative, \(A(x)\) is decreasing.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When \(\cos(x^2) = 0\text{,}\) \(A(x)\) has a maximum or a minimum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The slope of the graph of \(y = A(x)\) at any point \(x = X\) is equal to the height of the graph \(y = \cos(x^2)\) at that point.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In summary, the lower curve is the integral of the upper one, and the upper curve is the derivative of the lower one.
    🔗
     To get a better idea of the simplicity and power of this approach to integrals, let’s look at another example:
    \begin{equation*} A(X) = \int_1^X \ln{x} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    The function \(A\) is the solution to the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} y' = \ln{x}, \qquad y(1) = 0; \end{equation*}
    we find it, as usual, by Euler’s method. The graphs \(y = \ln{x}\) and \(y = A(x)\) are shown at the left. Notice once again that the height of the graph of \(A(x)\) at any point \(x = X\) equals the area under the graph of \(y = \ln{x}\) from \(x = 1\) to \(x = X\text{.}\) Also, the graph of \(A\) becomes steeper as the height of \(\ln{x}\) increases. In particular, the graph of \(A\) is horizontal (at \(x = 1\)) when \(\ln{x} = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.4.3 Antiderivatives

    We have developed a

    Aside
    Find an integral…
    🔗
    novel approach to integration in this section. We start by replacing a given integral by an accumulation function:
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \qquad \leadsto \qquad A(X) = \int_a^X f(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    Then we try to find \(A(X)\text{.}\) If we do, then the original integral is just the value of \(A\) at \(X = b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    At first glance, this doesn’t seem to be a sensible approach. We appear to be making the problem harder: instead of searching for a single number, we must now find an entire function. However, we know that \(y =A(x)\) solves the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} y' = f(x), \qquad y(a) = 0. \end{equation*}
    This means we can use the complete ‘bag of tools’

    Aside
    …by solving a differential equation …
    🔗
    we have for solving differential equations to find \(A\text{.}\) The real advantage of the new approach is that it reduces integration to the fundamental activity of calculus—solving differential equations.
    🔗
    The differential equation \(y' = f(x)\) that arises in integration problems is special. The right hand side depends only on the input variable \(x\text{.}\) We studied this differential equation

    Aside
    …using antidifferentiation
    🔗
    in chapter 4.5, where we developed a special method to solve it—antidifferentiation.
    🔗
    We say that \(F(x)\) is an antiderivative of \(f(x)\) if \(f\) is the derivative of \(F\text{:}\) \(F'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\) Here are some examples (from page ):
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{cc} \mbox{function} & \mbox{antiderivative} \\[2pt] \hline \rule{0pt}{16pt} 5x^4 - 2x^3\rule{0pt}{16pt} & x^5 - \tfrac{1}{2} x^4 \\[6pt] 5x^4 - 2x^3 + 17x & x^5 - \tfrac{1}{2} x^4 + \tfrac{17}{2} x^2 \\[6pt] 6 \cdot 10^z + 17/z^5 & 6 \cdot 10^z/\ln{10} - 17/4z^4 \\[6pt] 3\sin{t} - 2t^3 & -3\cos{t} - \tfrac{1}{2} t^4 \\[6pt] \pi\cos{x} + \pi^2 & \pi\sin{x} + \pi^2 x \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Since \(y = A(x)\) solves the differential equation \(y' = f(x)\text{,}\)

    Aside
    \(A\) is an antiderivative of \(f\)
    🔗
    we have \(A'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\) Thus, \(A(x)\) is an antiderivative of \(f(x)\text{,}\) so we can try to find \(A\) by antidifferentiating \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Here is an example. Suppose we want to find
    \begin{equation*} A(X) = \int_2^X 5x^4 - 2x^3 \, dx. \end{equation*}
    Notice that \(f(x) = 5x^4 - 2x^3\) is the first function in the previous table. Since \(A\) must be an antiderivative of \(f\text{,}\) let’s try the antiderivative for \(f\) that we find in the table:
    \begin{equation*} F(x) = x^5 - \tfrac{1}{2} x^4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The problem is that \(F\) must also

    Aside
    Check the condition \(A(2) = 0\)
    🔗
    satisfy the initial condition \(F(2) = 0\text{.}\) However,
    \begin{equation*} F(2) = 2^5 - \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2^4 = 32 - \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 16 = 24 \neq 0, \end{equation*}
    so the initial condition does not hold for this particular choice of antiderivative. But this problem is easy to fix. Let
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = F(x) - 24. \end{equation*}
    Since \(A(x)\) differs from \(F(x)\) only by a constant, it has the same derivative—namely, \(5x^4 - 2x^3\text{.}\) So \(A(x)\) is still an antiderivative of \(5x^4 - 2x^3\text{.}\) But it also satisfies the initial condition:
    \begin{equation*} A(2) = F(2) - 24 = 24 - 24 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Therefore \(A(x)\) solves the problem; it has the right derivative and the right value at \(x = 2\text{.}\) Thus we have a formula for the accumulation function
    \begin{equation*} \int_2^X 5x^4 - 2x^3 \, dx = X^5 - \tfrac{1}{2} X^4 - 24. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The key step in finding the correct accumulation function \(A\) was to recognize that a given function \(f\) has infinitely many antiderivatives: if \(F\) is an antiderivative, then so is \(F + C\text{,}\) for any constant \(C\text{.}\) The general procedure for finding an accumulation function involves these two steps:

    Example..

    To illustrate the procedure, let’s find the accumulation function
    \begin{equation*} A(X) = \int_0^X \sin{x} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    The first step is to find an antiderivative for \(f(x) = \sin{x}\text{.}\) A natural choice is \(F(x) = -\cos{x}\text{.}\) To carry out the second step, note that \(a = 0\text{.}\) Since \(F(0) = -\cos{0} = -1\text{,}\) we set
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = F(x) - F(0) = -\cos{x} - (-1) = 1 - \cos{x}. \end{equation*}
    The graphs \(y = \sin{x}\) and \(y = 1 - \cos{x}\) are shown below.
    🔗
    Now that we have a
    Aside
    Evaluating specific integrals
    🔗
    formula for \(A(x)\) we can find the exact value of integrals involving \(\sin{x}\text{.}\) For instance,
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\pi} \sin{x} \, dx = A(\pi) = 1 - \cos{\pi} = 1 - (-1) = 2. \end{equation*}
    Also,
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin{x} \, dx = A(\pi/2) = 1 - \cos{\pi/2} = 1 - (0) = 1, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi} \sin{x} \, dx = A(2\pi) = 1 - \cos{2\pi} = 1 - (1) = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    These values are exact, and we got them without calculating Riemann sums. However, we have already found the value of the third integral. On page  we argued from the shape of the graph \(y = \sin{x}\) that the signed area between \(x = 0\) and \(x = 2\pi\) must be 0. That argument is no help in finding the area to \(x = \pi\) or to \(x = \pi/2\text{,}\) though. Before the fundamental theorem showed us we could evaluate integrals by finding antiderivatives, we could only make estimates using Riemann sums. Here, for example, are midpoint Riemann sums for
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin{x} \, dx: \qquad \begin{tabular}{cc} subintervals & Riemann sum \\[1pt] \hline \begin{tabular}{r} \rule{0pt}{14pt}10\\100\\1000\\10000 \end{tabular} & \begin{tabular}{l} 1.001\,028\,868 \rule{0pt}{14pt} \\ 1.000\,010\,325 \\ 1.000\,000\,147 \\ 1.000\,000\,045 \end{tabular} \end{tabular} \end{equation*}
    According to the table, the value of the integral is 1.000000…, to six decimal places accuracy.
    Aside
    The fundamental theorem makes absolute precision possible
    🔗
    That is valuable information, and is often as accurate as we need. However, the fundamental theorem tells us that the value of the integral is 1 exactly! With the new approach, we can achieve absolute precision.
    🔗
    Precision is the result of having a formula for the antiderivative. Notice how we used that formula to express the value of the integral. Starting with an arbitrary antiderivative \(F(x)\) of \(f(x)\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} A(X) = \int_a^X f(x) \, dx = F(X) - F(a). \end{equation*}
    If we set \(X = b\) we find
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a). \end{equation*}
    In other words,
    🔗
    If \(F(x)\) is any antiderivative of \(f(x)\text{,}\)
    🔗
    then \({\displaystyle \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Example. Evaluate \({\displaystyle \int_0^{\ln{2}} e^{-u} \, du.}\)
    🔗
    For the antiderivative, we can choose \(F(u) = -e^{-u}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} int_0^{\ln{2}} e^{-u} \, du \amp= F(\ln{2}) - F(0)\\ \amp= -e^{-\ln{2}} - (-e^{0}) = -(1/2) - (-1) = 1/2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Example. Evaluate \({\displaystyle \int_2^3 \ln{t} \, dt.}\)
    🔗
    For the antiderivative, we can choose \(F(t) = t \ln{t} - t\text{.}\) Using the product rule, we can show that this is indeed an antiderivative:
    \begin{equation*} F'(t) = t \cdot \dfrac{1}{t} + 1 \cdot \ln{t} - 1 = 1 + \ln{t} - 1 = \ln{t} \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    \begin{align*} int_2^3 \ln{t} \, dt \amp= F(3) - F(2)\\ \amp= 3 \cdot \ln{3} - 3 - (2 \cdot \ln{2} - 2)\\ \amp= \ln{3^3} - \ln{2^2} - 1 = \ln{27} - \ln{4} - 1 = \ln(27/4) - 1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    While formulas make it possible to get exact values, they do present us with problems of their own. For instance, we need to know that \(t \ln{t} - t\) is an antiderivative of \(\ln{t}\text{.}\) This is not obvious. In fact, there is no guarantee that the antiderivative of a function given by a formula will have a formula! The antiderivatives of \(\cos(x^2)\) and \(\sin(x)/x\) do not have formulas, for instance. Many techniques have been devised to find the formula for an antiderivative. In chapter 11 of Calculus II we will survey some of those that are most frequently used.

    Parameters.

    In chapter 4.2 we considered differential equations that involved parameters (see pages –).
    Aside
    When integrals depend on parameters …
    🔗
    It also happens that integrals can involve parameters. However, parameters complicate numerical work. If we calculate the value of an integral numerically, by making estimates with Riemann sums, we must first fix the value of any parameters that appear. This makes it difficult to see how the value of the integral depends on the parameters. We would have to give new values to the parameters and then recalculate the Riemann sums.
    🔗
    The outcome is much simpler
    Aside
    …the fundamental theorem can help make the relation clearer
    🔗
    and more transparent if we are able to use the fundamental theorem to get a formula for the integral. The parameters just appear in the formula, so it is immediately clear how the integral depends on the parameters.
    🔗
    Here is an example that we shall explore further later. We want to see how the integrals
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \sin(\alpha x) \, dx \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \int_a^b \cos(\alpha x) \, dx \end{equation*}
    depend on the parameter \(\alpha \neq 0\text{,}\) and also on the parameters \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\) To begin, you should check that \(F(x) = -\cos(\alpha x)/\alpha\) is an antiderivative of \(\sin(\alpha x)\text{.}\) Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \sin(\alpha x) \, dx = \dfrac{-\cos(\alpha b)}{\alpha} - \dfrac{-\cos(\alpha a)}{\alpha} = \dfrac{\cos(\alpha a) -\cos(\alpha b)}{\alpha}. \end{equation*}
    In a similar way, you should be able to show that
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \cos(\alpha x) \, dx = \dfrac{\sin(\alpha b) - \sin(\alpha a)}{\alpha}. \end{equation*}
    Suppose the interval \([a, b]\) is exactly one-half of a full period: \([0, \pi/\alpha]\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} int_0^{\pi/\alpha} \sin(\alpha x) \, dx \amp= \dfrac{\cos(\alpha \cdot 0) -\cos(\alpha \pi/\alpha)}{\alpha}\\ \amp= \dfrac{\cos{0} - \cos{\pi}}{\alpha} = \dfrac{1 - (-1)}{\alpha} = \dfrac{2}{\alpha}. \end{align*}
